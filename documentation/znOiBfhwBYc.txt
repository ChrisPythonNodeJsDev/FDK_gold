So what exactly is the Power of Three framework? Before we explore the concept, there is an&nbsp; important clarification we need to make about&nbsp;&nbsp; the Power of Three, also known as AMD, especially&nbsp; when we relate it to proper Wyckoff methodology. The Power of Three was popularized by&nbsp; ICT, and the accumulation, manipulation,&nbsp;&nbsp; and distribution naming was simplified to make the&nbsp; concept easier to understand. Here at ComLucro,&nbsp;&nbsp; we are students of Wyckoff methodology, and we&nbsp; even have dedicated content and a full course&nbsp;&nbsp; on this subject on the channel, so we prefer&nbsp; to use the correct Wyckoff nomenclature. The&nbsp;&nbsp; strategy itself does not change at all, only&nbsp; the terminology used to describe the phases. It is important to understand&nbsp; that during a consolidation,&nbsp;&nbsp; you never know in real time whether it will&nbsp; result in accumulation or distribution. A&nbsp;&nbsp; consolidation only reveals its true nature&nbsp; after the next phase unfolds. Because of that,&nbsp;&nbsp; labeling a range too early as accumulation&nbsp; or distribution can be misleading. The correct way to think about it is that&nbsp; consolidation is neutral until the market shows&nbsp;&nbsp; its intention through continuation or reversal.&nbsp; What changes is the probability based on context. In a bullish environment, consolidations tend to&nbsp;&nbsp; act as accumulation or reaccumulation before&nbsp; continuation higher. In a bearish environment,&nbsp;&nbsp; consolidations tend to function as&nbsp; distribution before continuation lower. This mindset keeps your expectations aligned&nbsp; with market structure, avoids rigid labeling,&nbsp;&nbsp; and allows you to apply the Power of Three with&nbsp; clarity, flexibility, and proper Wyckoff logic. Now, let's take a closer look at the Power&nbsp; of Three framework. The Power of Three is a&nbsp;&nbsp; trading pattern that divides price action&nbsp; into three major phases: consolidation,&nbsp;&nbsp; manipulation, and continuation.&nbsp; The first phase is consolidation,&nbsp;&nbsp; which initially appears as a neutral&nbsp; range and may later reveal itself as&nbsp;&nbsp; either accumulation or distribution, depending&nbsp; on how price behaves in the following phase. During this phase, price lacks a clear&nbsp; directional trend and often forms equal&nbsp;&nbsp; highs and equal lows, building liquidity&nbsp; and stop losses above and below the range,&nbsp;&nbsp; depending on the time frame. This&nbsp; consolidation can appear in different&nbsp;&nbsp; structures, and on higher time frames&nbsp; it usually forms a well defined range. The manipulation phase occurs when price moves&nbsp; away from the consolidation through what appears&nbsp;&nbsp; to be a breakout, but fails to follow through.&nbsp; In basic price action, when price breaks below a&nbsp;&nbsp; support level, that level often becomes resistance&nbsp; and pushes price lower due to trader behavior. Traders who previously went long see&nbsp; the retest as an opportunity to exit,&nbsp;&nbsp; while others see it as a chance to enter short.&nbsp; However, when price fails to continue and quickly&nbsp;&nbsp; returns back inside the consolidation range,&nbsp; this movement is classified as a fake out. This action is designed to sweep liquidity&nbsp; outside the range and trap breakout traders.&nbsp;&nbsp; Once liquidity has been collected, price typically&nbsp; expands in the opposite direction. This final&nbsp;&nbsp; phase is what we refer to as continuation,&nbsp; and it is where trade execution takes place. As traders applying Smart Money Concepts,&nbsp; the objective is to enter after liquidity&nbsp;&nbsp; has been taken and stops have&nbsp; been triggered. In simple terms,&nbsp;&nbsp; the Power of Three framework describes a sequence&nbsp; of consolidation, manipulation, and continuation,&nbsp;&nbsp; with accumulation or distribution only becoming&nbsp; clear after the continuation phase unfolds. Similarly, in a bearish scenario, the&nbsp; market forms a consolidation followed&nbsp;&nbsp; by a breakout to the upside, which engages&nbsp; liquidity above the equal highs. If price&nbsp;&nbsp; quickly returns back inside the&nbsp; range, this signals a fake out,&nbsp;&nbsp; and the Power of Three pattern completes with&nbsp; a sharp continuation move to the downside. So, how do we enter a trade using&nbsp; the Power of Three pattern? We aim&nbsp;&nbsp; to enter trades after the manipulation&nbsp; phase is confirmed. First, price must&nbsp;&nbsp; return back inside the consolidation&nbsp; range, confirming the failed breakout. We then mark the candle closes that define the&nbsp; consolidation area. For entry confirmation,&nbsp;&nbsp; we need a clear break and close back&nbsp; inside the box. The stop loss is placed&nbsp;&nbsp; above the swing high, while targets&nbsp; are set at key levels ahead of price. There are situations where price expands&nbsp; aggressively and the initial move is missed. When&nbsp;&nbsp; that happens, we wait for a new market structure&nbsp; to form and then look for a pullback to enter at&nbsp;&nbsp; a better price with a balanced risk to reward&nbsp; ratio. In other words, we focus on continuation&nbsp;&nbsp; trades after manipulation, aligning with the&nbsp; dominant direction rather than chasing price. If we look at this price formation&nbsp; from an intraday perspective,&nbsp;&nbsp; the daily candlestick would reflect this&nbsp; behavior clearly. Price opens, forms a high,&nbsp;&nbsp; moves to a low, and then closes strong.&nbsp; The objective is to capture the largest&nbsp;&nbsp; directional move within the intraday&nbsp; range using the Power of Three framework.
